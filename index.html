Training Agents in Simulation: How Numerical Integrators Influence Learned Behavior

A study of timestep and solver effects in reinforcement learning environments

Abstract

Reinforcement learning agents are commonly trained in simulation under the assumption that learned behavior is largely invariant to simulator configuration. However, because simulated environments are generated through numerical integration, timestep size and integrator selection directly influence the dynamics experienced during training. This project investigates how simulation configuration affects learned reinforcement learning behavior across classical control environments. Multiple numerical integrators, including Forward Euler, Symplectic Euler, Velocity Verlet, RK2, RK4, and Implicit Euler, were evaluated across a range of timestep values using DQN and PPO agents in Acrobot and Cartpole environments. Results demonstrate a complex relationship between reinforcement learning algorithms and simulator configuration. Training stability, convergence behavior, and transfer performance varied substantially across timesteps and integrators, suggesting that agents may partially adapt to numerical artifacts introduced by the simulator itself. These findings motivate further investigation into simulation aware training and simulator configuration as a potential domain randomization technique.

Motivation

One day I was having a discussing with a colleague about training agents in simulation in which he had the following opinion: the agent has no notion of timestep . More concretely, training the agent in a simulator with a timestep such as \(10^{-3}\) should not, by itself, determine how the agent behaves during real-world deployment.

However, as someone with experience in multibody dynamics and simulation, this claim did not sit well with me. Even if timestep is not explicitly embedded in the learning algorithm, there is still an implicit influnce simply because learned behavior is contingent on the environment, and the environment is contingent on what I refer to as the simulation configuration.

This simulation configuration refers to both the timestep and numerical integrator. Hence, there is not necessarily a clean separation between learned agent behavior and timestep. Actually, it is even more complex than initially considered since we must also consider the choice of integrator.

Thus, I wanted to take a moment to invesitgate how simulation configuration can effect learned agent behavior, and see what insights I could gather from my experiments.
