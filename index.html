Training Agents in Simulation: How Numerical Integrators Influence Learned Behavior

A study of timestep and solver effects in reinforcement learning environments

Abstract

Reinforcement learning agents are commonly trained in simulation under the assumption that learned behavior is largely invariant to simulator configuration. However, because simulated environments are generated through numerical integration, timestep size and integrator selection directly influence the dynamics experienced during training. This project investigates how simulation configuration affects learned reinforcement learning behavior across classical control environments. Multiple numerical integrators, including Forward Euler, Symplectic Euler, Velocity Verlet, RK2, RK4, and Implicit Euler, were evaluated across a range of timestep values using DQN and PPO agents in Acrobot and Cartpole environments. Results demonstrate a complex relationship between reinforcement learning algorithms and simulator configuration. Training stability, convergence behavior, and transfer performance varied substantially across timesteps and integrators, suggesting that agents may partially adapt to numerical artifacts introduced by the simulator itself. These findings motivate further investigation into simulation aware training and simulator configuration as a potential domain randomization technique.

Motivation

One day I was having a discussing with a colleague about training agents in simulation in which he had the following opinion: the agent has no notion of timestep . More concretely, training the agent in a simulator with a timestep such as \(10^{-3}\) should not, by itself, determine how the agent behaves during real-world deployment.

However, as someone with experience in multibody dynamics and simulation, this claim did not sit well with me. Even if timestep is not explicitly embedded in the learning algorithm, there is still an implicit influnce simply because learned behavior is contingent on the environment, and the environment is contingent on what I refer to as the simulation configuration.

This simulation configuration refers to both the timestep and numerical integrator. Hence, there is not necessarily a clean separation between learned agent behavior and timestep. Actually, it is even more complex than initially considered since we must also consider the choice of integrator.

Thus, I wanted to take a moment to invesitgate how simulation configuration can effect learned agent behavior, and see what insights I could gather from my experiments.

Methodology

The central goal of this project is to evaluate whether the choice of numerical integrator and simulator timestep affects the policies learned by reinforcement learning agents. To isolate this effect, all other aspects of training were held constant across runs. The only variables that changed between experiments were the integrator type and the timestep \(\Delta t\).

Environments

Experiments were conducted on two classic control tasks from OpenAI Gym: CartPole and Acrobot. Both environments involve continuous physical dynamics and discrete action spaces, making them well-suited for studying the effect of numerical integration on RL training.

CartPole requires an agent to balance a pole mounted on a cart by applying left or right forces to the cart. The state consists of cart position, cart velocity, pole angle, and pole angular velocity. The episode ends if the pole falls beyond a threshold angle or the cart moves out of bounds.

Acrobot requires an agent to swing a two-link pendulum above a target height by applying a torque at the joint connecting the two links. The state consists of the sine and cosine of the two joint angles and their angular velocities. The episode ends when the tip of the second link reaches the required height.

These two tasks differ in their physical structure and reward geometry. CartPole is a stabilization task with a dense reward signal, while Acrobot is a swing-up task with a sparse reward signal. Using both environments allows the results to be assessed across different control challenges.

Algorithms

Two reinforcement learning algorithms were evaluated: DQN and PPO. Both algorithms were applied to every integrator-timestep combination in both environments, resulting in a fully crossed experimental design.

DQN is an off-policy, value-based algorithm that learns an action-value function approximated by a neural network. It maintains a replay buffer of past transitions and uses a separate target network to stabilize the Bellman update targets. Because DQN stores and reuses past experience, it is more sensitive to the distribution of transitions collected during training.

PPO is an on-policy, policy-gradient algorithm that directly learns a parameterized policy. It discards experience after each update and relies only on the most recently collected trajectories. Its clipped objective prevents large policy changes in a single update step. Because PPO throws away past experience, it is less sensitive to the history of transitions and more sensitive to the quality of the current rollout.

Integrators and Timesteps

Six numerical integrators were implemented and evaluated. The non-symplectic integrators, ordered from lowest to highest fidelity, were Forward Euler, Implicit Euler, RK2, and RK4. The symplectic integrators were Symplectic Euler and Verlet. Each integrator replaced the default numerical integration in the physics step of both environments.

Each integrator was evaluated across seven timestep values:

\[ \Delta t \in \{10^{-2},\; 5 \times 10^{-2},\; 10^{-1},\; 0.5,\; 1,\; 1.5,\; 2\} \]

This range spans two orders of magnitude, from timesteps small enough that all integrators should behave similarly, to timesteps large enough that low-order methods are expected to produce substantially inaccurate trajectories.

The full experiment therefore consisted of

\[ 2 \text{ environments} \times 2 \text{ algorithms} \times 6 \text{ integrators} \times 7 \text{ timesteps} = 168 \text{ training runs} \]

Controlling for Simulation Time

A critical methodological concern is that different timestep configurations produce a different number of environment steps per unit of simulated time. Smaller timesteps take more steps to simulate the same physical duration. Without any correction, a small-\(\Delta t\) agent would observe more transitions, collect more reward, and receive more gradient updates per episode than a large-\(\Delta t\) agent, making direct comparison misleading.

To address this, the number of environment steps per episode was scaled so that every configuration simulates the same total physical duration \(T_{\text{episode}}\).

\[ N_{\text{steps}} = \frac{T_{\text{episode}}}{\Delta t} \]

This ensures that regardless of timestep, every episode represents the same amount of simulated time.

Reward Scaling

Because smaller timesteps produce more steps per episode, an agent trained with a small \(\Delta t\) would accumulate more reward steps than a large-\(\Delta t\) agent even if both simulated the same physical duration. To make total episode returns comparable across timestep configurations, rewards were scaled by \(\Delta t\) at each step.

\[ r_t^{\text{scaled}} = \Delta t \cdot r_t \]

With this scaling, the total return accumulated over an episode approximates an integral of reward over simulated time rather than a sum over environment steps. Two agents that behave identically on the same physical trajectory should therefore receive the same total return regardless of which \(\Delta t\) they use.

DQN-Specific Controls

Two additional adjustments were made for DQN to account for the change in step count across timestep configurations.

The replay buffer warmup was scaled by \(\Delta t\) so that training did not begin until the buffer contained experience covering a fixed amount of simulated time, rather than a fixed number of raw environment steps.

\[ N_{\text{warmup}} = N_{\text{warmup episodes}} \cdot \frac{T_{\text{episode}}}{\Delta t} \]

The neural network update frequency was also scaled by \(\Delta t\) so that gradient updates occurred at a consistent rate relative to simulated time rather than relative to raw step count.

\[ N_{\text{update interval}} = \max\!\left(1,\; \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{T_{\text{update}}}{\Delta t}\right)\right) \]

Together, these controls ensure that DQN configurations with different timesteps receive comparable amounts of simulated-time experience before and during learning, so that observed differences in performance can be attributed to the integrator and timestep rather than to differences in training exposure.

Evaluation

All training runs were conducted for the same number of total episodes. Agent performance was measured by the mean episode return over a fixed evaluation window at the end of training. Results were compared across integrators and timesteps within each algorithm-environment combination to assess how numerical configuration affects the final quality of the learned policy.

Beyond completing the domain randomization experiments, there are several natural extensions that this work motivates.

Physical hardware validation

A central limitation of this project is that all evaluation was performed in simulation. Policies were trained and tested on the same simulated environment, which means that the sim-to-real transfer problem was never directly confronted. A meaningful next step would be to deploy learned policies on physical CartPole and Acrobot hardware and measure how much real-world performance degrades relative to simulated performance. This would turn the numerical integrator comparison into a genuine sim-to-real study, since policies trained under high-fidelity or symplectic integrators may transfer more reliably to physical hardware precisely because their training dynamics more accurately reflect true conservative mechanics.

More complex environments

CartPole and Acrobot are useful benchmarks but are relatively low-dimensional systems. Scaling this investigation to MuJoCo environments, which involve higher-dimensional state spaces, contact dynamics, and more complex reward structures, would test whether the relationships observed here generalize beyond simple pendulum-like systems. MuJoCo's physics engine also exposes timestep and integrator configuration directly, making it a natural platform for extending this line of work. It is an open question whether the advantage of symplectic integrators or the sensitivity to timestep observed here persists at the complexity of locomotion or manipulation tasks.
