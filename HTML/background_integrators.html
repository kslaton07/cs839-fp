Background: Numerical Integrators

A numerical integrator approximates the continuous solution of a differential equation by advancing the system state in discrete time steps of size \(\Delta t\). For a system described by

\[ \dot{y} = f(t, y), \quad y(t_0) = y_0 \]

an integrator produces a sequence of states \(y_0, y_1, y_2, \ldots\) that approximates the true solution \(y(t)\) at times \(t_0, t_0 + \Delta t, t_0 + 2\Delta t, \ldots\). Every integrator makes assumptions about how the state changes within a single step, and those assumptions determine both the accuracy of the approximation and whether certain physical properties of the system are preserved.

The integrators used in this project fall into two categories: non-symplectic and symplectic. Non-symplectic integrators are general-purpose methods that do not preserve the geometric structure of Hamiltonian systems. Symplectic integrators are specifically designed for Hamiltonian mechanics and preserve the symplectic structure of the phase space exactly, which has important consequences for long-run energy behavior.

Non-Symplectic Integrators

Forward Euler

Forward Euler is the simplest explicit integrator. Its core assumption is that the derivative \(f(t, y)\) remains constant throughout the entire step, equal to its value at the beginning of the step. The update rule is

\[ y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t \, f(t_n, y_n) \]

Comparing this to a Taylor expansion of the true solution,

\[ y(t_n + \Delta t) = y(t_n) + \Delta t \, \dot{y}(t_n) + \frac{\Delta t^2}{2} \ddot{y}(t_n) + \cdots \]

Forward Euler matches the first two terms and discards everything of order \(\Delta t^2\) and higher. The local truncation error per step is therefore \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\), and the global error accumulated over a fixed interval is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\), making it a first-order method.

For conservative mechanical systems, the constant-derivative assumption systematically overestimates the total energy, causing the trajectory to spiral outward in phase space. This energy drift worsens over time and can lead to unstable behavior in long simulations.

Implicit Euler

Implicit Euler makes the opposite assumption to Forward Euler: it treats the derivative as constant throughout the step but evaluates it at the end of the step rather than the beginning. The update rule is

\[ y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t \, f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1}) \]

Because \(y_{n+1}\) appears on both sides, this is an implicit equation that typically requires solving a nonlinear system at each step. The local truncation error is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\) and the global error is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\), so Implicit Euler is also first-order accurate.

Unlike Forward Euler, the end-of-step evaluation produces artificial numerical dissipation. The method systematically underestimates the energy of the system, causing the trajectory to spiral inward in phase space. While this dissipation prevents the blow-up seen with Forward Euler, it introduces a different form of systematic error: energy is removed from the system at a rate that depends on \(\Delta t\).

Runge-Kutta 2 (RK2)

Runge-Kutta methods improve accuracy by evaluating the derivative at multiple points within a single step and forming a weighted combination of those evaluations. The second-order Runge-Kutta method (midpoint method) uses two evaluations. It first takes a half-step using Forward Euler to estimate the midpoint derivative, then uses that midpoint derivative to advance the full step.

\[ k_1 = f(t_n, y_n) \]
\[ k_2 = f\!\left(t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2},\; y_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} k_1\right) \]
\[ y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t \, k_2 \]

The assumption is that the midpoint derivative is a better representative of the average rate of change over the interval than the initial derivative alone. This cancels the leading-order error of Forward Euler, giving a local truncation error of \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^3)\) and a global error of \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\): a second-order method. RK2 does not preserve energy in conservative systems, but its energy drift is smaller than Forward Euler for the same step size.

Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4)

RK4 is the classical fourth-order method. It evaluates the derivative at four points within the step (the start, two midpoints, and the end) and combines them with weights derived from Simpson's rule.

\[ k_1 = f(t_n,\; y_n) \]
\[ k_2 = f\!\left(t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2},\; y_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} k_1\right) \]
\[ k_3 = f\!\left(t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2},\; y_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2} k_2\right) \]
\[ k_4 = f\!\left(t_n + \Delta t,\; y_n + \Delta t \, k_3\right) \]
\[ y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{\Delta t}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right) \]

The assumption is that a Simpson-weighted average of these four slope estimates approximates the integral of \(f\) over the interval far more accurately than any single evaluation. The local truncation error is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^5)\) and the global error is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)\). RK4 can therefore use much larger step sizes than Forward Euler to achieve the same accuracy, but each step requires four evaluations of \(f\) rather than one. Like all Runge-Kutta methods, RK4 is non-symplectic and will still exhibit slow energy drift in very long simulations of conservative systems.

Symplectic Integrators

Many physical systems are Hamiltonian: their dynamics can be described by a Hamiltonian function \(H(q, p)\) where \(q\) is the generalized position and \(p\) is the generalized momentum. The equations of motion are

\[ \dot{q} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot{p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} \]

A key property of Hamiltonian flow is that it is symplectic: it exactly preserves the symplectic two-form \(\omega = dq \wedge dp\), which corresponds physically to conservation of phase-space volume (Liouville's theorem) and, over long time horizons, near-conservation of energy.

Non-symplectic integrators do not preserve this structure. Their numerical trajectories live on a slightly different effective Hamiltonian that drifts away from the true Hamiltonian over time, causing secular energy error. Symplectic integrators, by contrast, exactly satisfy a modified Hamiltonian that is \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^p)\) close to the true one. As a result, energy oscillates around the true value rather than drifting monotonically, which makes symplectic methods much better suited to long simulations of conservative mechanical systems.

Symplectic Euler

Symplectic Euler is the simplest symplectic integrator. It resembles Forward Euler but breaks the position and momentum updates apart so that one uses the updated value of the other. For a separable Hamiltonian \(H(q,p) = T(p) + V(q)\), the update is

\[ p_{n+1} = p_n - \Delta t \, \frac{\partial V}{\partial q}(q_n) \]
\[ q_{n+1} = q_n + \Delta t \, \frac{\partial T}{\partial p}(p_{n+1}) \]

The key assumption is the sequential update: the momentum is advanced first using the old position, then the position is advanced using the new momentum. This asymmetry is what makes the method symplectic. Symplectic Euler is first-order accurate (global error \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\)), so its per-step approximation is no better than Forward Euler, but it does not accumulate systematic energy error. Energy oscillates around its true value by an amount proportional to \(\Delta t\) rather than drifting.

Verlet

The Verlet integrator (specifically the velocity Verlet form) is a second-order symplectic method. It derives from assuming that the force (and therefore acceleration) is approximately constant over a half-step, and uses a staggered update for positions and velocities.

\[ q_{n+1} = q_n + \Delta t \, v_n + \frac{\Delta t^2}{2} a_n \]
\[ a_{n+1} = -\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}(q_{n+1}) \]
\[ v_{n+1} = v_n + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\left(a_n + a_{n+1}\right) \]

Here \(a_n\) is the acceleration at step \(n\), computed from the force at position \(q_n\). The position update uses the second-order Taylor expansion (the half-step acceleration assumption), and the velocity update averages the accelerations at the start and end of the step. This averaging is what gives Verlet its second-order accuracy: global error \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\).

Verlet is also time-reversible: replacing \(\Delta t\) with \(-\Delta t\) and running the integrator backward exactly retraces the forward trajectory. This time-reversibility is another geometric property of Hamiltonian flow that non-symplectic methods do not preserve. Combined with its symplectic character, Verlet provides substantially better long-run energy conservation than RK2, despite having the same formal order of accuracy.

Connection to This Project

In this project, the environment simulator uses a numerical integrator to advance the physical state of the system at each decision step. The integrator is therefore part of the training environment seen by the RL agent. Different integrators produce different trajectories from the same initial condition, which means the agent observes different state sequences, receives different rewards, and may encounter different termination behavior depending on which integrator is used.

Non-symplectic integrators such as Forward Euler can introduce artificial energy gain that makes the system appear more unstable than it truly is. Implicit Euler introduces artificial damping that may make the task appear easier to control. Symplectic integrators preserve the conservative character of the system more faithfully, but their qualitative advantage over high-order non-symplectic methods such as RK4 depends on the length of the trajectory and the sensitivity of the reward to energy errors. These differences motivate comparing trained policies across integrators, rather than treating the simulator as a neutral tool whose numerical details are irrelevant.
