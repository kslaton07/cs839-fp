Results: PPO on Acrobot

Overview

The broad trends observed with DQN on Acrobot carry over to PPO: Forward Euler benefits from artificial energy injection, Implicit Euler is hampered by its overdamping, and intermediate timesteps generally outperform the extremes. The consistency of these trends across two fundamentally different learning algorithms is itself a meaningful result, and it is discussed further below. The heatmap of final returns across all configurations is shown first.

Universal Failure at the Smallest Timestep

One pattern that stands out more clearly in the PPO results is that nearly every integrator performs extremely poorly at \(\Delta t = 0.01\), including Forward Euler, which was otherwise a strong performer. This is not a coincidence, and it is not caused by the integrator. It is a consequence of how the discount factor interacts with the effective planning horizon at very small timesteps.

With a fixed discount factor \(\gamma = 0.99\) per environment step, the effective planning horizon of the agent is approximately

\[ \frac{1}{1 - \gamma} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.99} = 100 \text{ steps} \]

At \(\Delta t = 0.01\), those 100 steps correspond to only one second of simulated time. Acrobot requires the agent to plan a swing-up maneuver that unfolds over several seconds. The reward for reaching the target height is so heavily discounted by the time it arrives that it contributes almost nothing to the value estimates seen during training. Every state looks approximately equally hopeless from the agent's perspective, advantages collapse toward zero, and PPO has essentially no gradient signal to differentiate good actions from bad ones. The result is that all integrators fail at this timestep for the same reason, regardless of their numerical properties.

This is a known issue in deep RL with fixed discount factors: the same \(\gamma\) that works well at one timestep can completely blind the agent at a finer timestep. A principled fix would be to scale \(\gamma\) with \(\Delta t\) so that the effective planning horizon in simulated time remains constant across configurations.

Algorithm-Indifferent Trends

The fact that DQN and PPO produce qualitatively similar performance patterns across integrators and timesteps is a notable finding in its own right. DQN is off-policy, value-based, and replay-buffer dependent. PPO is on-policy, policy-gradient based, and discards past experience after each update. These are very different inductive biases, yet the ranking of integrators and the general shape of performance versus timestep are broadly consistent between them.

This suggests that the dominant factor shaping learned behavior is not the RL algorithm itself, but rather the physics of the training environment as mediated by the integrator and timestep. The optimal policy for Acrobot is determined by the underlying task structure, and different simulator configurations either make that policy easier or harder to discover by introducing artificial energy dynamics. An integrator that adds energy helps both DQN and PPO. One that removes it hurts both. The algorithm moderates the rate and stability of learning, but the integrator shapes what there is to learn in the first place. What ultimately matters for deployment, however, is not which configuration learns fastest in simulation but which learned policy transfers most faithfully to the real system, which is addressed in the transfer evaluation below.

Transfer Evaluation

Each trained PPO policy was evaluated on the same ground truth environment used for DQN: RK4 at \(\Delta t = 0.01\). The transfer heatmap is shown below.

PPO's transfer results are noticeably stronger than DQN's overall. Several configurations achieve transfer rewards in the range of \(-15\) to \(-25\), and the number of completely failed transfers (reward of \(-100\)) is lower. A likely explanation is the structural difference between what each algorithm actually learns. DQN learns a Q-function: a mapping from state-action pairs to expected return values that are calibrated to the specific reward magnitudes and discount structure of the training environment. When the simulator changes at evaluation time, those absolute value estimates may mis-rank actions because the expected returns under the new dynamics differ from what the Q-function was fitted to. PPO, by contrast, learns a direct policy parameterized as a probability distribution over actions. The policy is trained to improve relative to a baseline through advantage estimation, which is less sensitive to the absolute scale of returns. A policy that learned to swing the arm in a particular direction because that direction yielded better-than-average outcomes is more likely to remain useful under slightly different dynamics than a Q-function whose absolute value estimates depend on the precise reward trajectory.

One particularly interesting pattern is that Implicit Euler maintains reasonable transfer performance even at the largest timesteps (\(\Delta t = 1, 1.5, 2\)), a window where nearly all other integrators collapse to \(-100\). Implicit Euler's numerical dissipation, which hurts its in-training performance, may coincidentally produce more conservative and generalizable policies: an agent that has learned to swing up a pendulum that is fighting artificial damping must apply more deliberate and sustained actions, and that behavioral pattern may translate more robustly to the ground truth environment than policies that relied on energy-inflating artifacts.

As with the DQN transfer analysis, it bears repeating that this is not a true sim-to-real evaluation. The ground truth is still a simulated environment, and any conclusions about physical deployability would require validation on real hardware.
