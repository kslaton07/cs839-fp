Results: DQN on Acrobot

Overview

Meaningful learning was achieved on Acrobot across several integrator and timestep configurations. Acrobot is a relatively forgiving training environment: episodes do not terminate on the first mistake, and the agent can explore the state space more freely before the episode ends. This gives the replay buffer a chance to accumulate diverse, informative transitions across a range of system states. The results reveal a complex and configuration-dependent picture of performance, where the choice of integrator interacts with the physics of the task in non-obvious ways.

Forward Euler Outperforms at Small and Medium Timesteps

Forward Euler consistently achieves strong performance across small and medium timesteps, which at first appears surprising for the lowest-fidelity non-symplectic integrator. The explanation lies in the nature of the Acrobot task itself.

The optimal control strategy for Acrobot is related to pumping energy into the double pendulum system to raise the tip above the target height. The policy essentially needs to exploit the system's natural dynamics to accumulate enough mechanical energy. Forward Euler, as discussed in the background section, systematically overestimates energy at each step due to its constant-derivative assumption. This artificial energy injection acts as a tailwind for the task: the simulated pendulum gains energy faster than a physically accurate system would, making it easier for the agent to discover the high-reward region and receive the positive reinforcement needed to learn. The agent effectively learns a policy shaped around an environment that is slightly easier than reality, and that advantage is large enough to show up clearly in final returns.

Integrator Accuracy and Convergence at Large Timesteps

As \(\Delta t\) increases, the gap between Forward Euler and the other non-symplectic integrators narrows, and by the largest timesteps most of them reach comparable performance. The integrator accuracy plot below helps explain why.

At small timesteps, integrators are well separated by their theoretical convergence order: RK4 produces far smaller errors than RK2, which in turn outperforms Forward Euler and Symplectic Euler. However, at large timesteps all of the first and second-order methods cluster together at similarly high error levels. When every integrator is producing large truncation errors, the qualitative difference between them matters less to the RL agent than the overall character of the dynamics it experiences. As a result, the performance advantage of Forward Euler relative to higher-order methods tends to shrink at large \(\Delta t\), and the non-symplectic methods become more comparable in training outcome.

Interestingly, Forward Euler does extremely poorly at the largest tested timestep of \(\Delta t = 2\). Its artificial energy injection, which was an advantage at moderate timesteps, becomes so severe at \(\Delta t = 2\) that the simulated dynamics are completely unrealistic. The agent has effectively been trained on a system so different from a plausible physical pendulum that the learned policy is useless even within the same simulator.

Symplectic Euler Underperforms Despite Reasonable Accuracy

Symplectic Euler performs poorly across nearly all timestep configurations, which is puzzling at first given that it sits alongside Forward Euler on the accuracy curve and is theoretically a more principled integrator for Hamiltonian systems. The key is that integrator accuracy and integrator behavior are not the same thing.

Symplectic Euler does not gain energy like Forward Euler, nor does it conserve it exactly. At moderate to large timesteps it introduces numerical damping, systematically draining energy from the system. For a task that requires pumping energy into a pendulum to reach a target height, an integrator that removes energy is fundamentally at odds with what the task demands. The agent trained under Symplectic Euler is trying to learn to swing up a pendulum that is fighting against artificial dissipation, which requires more aggressive actions for the same result. At larger timesteps the overdamping grows more severe, which explains why Symplectic Euler's performance does not recover the way the other non-symplectic integrators do. This is a clear example of why raw truncation error is an incomplete picture: what matters for RL is not just how large the error is, but what kind of physical bias it introduces into the training environment.

DQN Learns Better at Larger Timesteps

Across most integrators, DQN tends to achieve better final returns when trained at larger timestep values. With a fixed episode duration \(T_{\text{episode}}\), a larger \(\Delta t\) means fewer environment steps per episode. Each individual action therefore has a larger effect on the system state, which gives the agent coarser but more impactful control authority. In a sparse-reward task like Acrobot, this can help the agent discover the goal state earlier in training, providing the positive reinforcement signal that drives Q-value learning. At very small timesteps, the agent must string together many more individually small actions to swing the pendulum to the target, which makes the credit assignment problem harder and slows convergence.

Transfer Evaluation

To assess how well policies trained under different simulator configurations generalize, each trained policy was evaluated on a ground truth environment using RK4 at \(\Delta t = 0.01\), the highest-fidelity configuration tested. The heatmap below shows the transfer reward for every training integrator and timestep combination.

The transfer results show that policies trained at intermediate timesteps, particularly around \(\Delta t = 0.1\), tend to transfer better to the ground truth environment than those trained at very small or very large timesteps. This is somewhat counterintuitive: one might expect that training at a small timestep close to the ground truth \(\Delta t = 0.01\) would produce the most transferable policy. However, at very small timesteps, agents appear to overfit to the specific numerical artifacts of their training integrator. The fine-grained dynamics produced by each integrator at small \(\Delta t\) are distinct from one another, and a policy tuned to exploit those subtleties does not necessarily generalize to a different integrator, even at the same timestep. At intermediate timesteps, the coarser dynamics may produce policies that are less finely tuned and therefore more robust when transferred.

It is important to note that this is not a true sim-to-real evaluation. The ground truth environment is still a simulation, and the RK4 \(\Delta t = 0.01\) configuration itself did not learn well during training, as seen in the per-timestep plots above. This means the transfer results are measuring how well policies transfer to a high-fidelity simulator, not to a physical system, and the baseline they are being compared against is not a strong performer in its own right. Any conclusions about real-world deployability would require physical hardware validation, which remains an open direction for future work.
