Results: DQN on CartPole

Overview

DQN did not successfully learn to balance the pole across any integrator or timestep configuration tested. The heatmap below shows final episode return for every integrator and timestep combination. The uniformly poor performance is not a tuning failure specific to one configuration; it reflects a structural mismatch between how DQN learns and what CartPole demands of an agent during training.

Why DQN Struggles on CartPole

DQN is an off-policy algorithm that learns by repeatedly sampling past transitions from a replay buffer and fitting a neural network to approximate the action-value function. Its ability to learn depends critically on the quality and diversity of the experience stored in that buffer. CartPole creates a particularly hostile environment for this kind of learning.

In the early stages of training, before the agent has learned anything useful, nearly every episode terminates almost immediately. Random or near-random actions cause the pole to fall within a handful of steps, which means the replay buffer is filled almost entirely with short, failed trajectories. DQN then trains on this buffer repeatedly, receiving a signal that says little more than "most actions in most states lead quickly to failure," with no counterexamples of sustained balance to learn from.

This problem is compounded by how CartPole's reward structure interacts with DQN's bootstrapped value estimates. DQN estimates the value of a state-action pair as

\[ y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^{-}}(s', a') \]

When episodes terminate within a few steps, the agent never observes the long-horizon returns that would make \(\max_{a'} Q_{\theta^{-}}(s', a')\) a meaningful quantity. The bootstrapped targets collapse toward zero, the Q-values learned from those targets are unreliable, and the policy derived from those Q-values does no better than random exploration. The agent is stuck: it cannot collect better experience without a better policy, and it cannot develop a better policy without better experience.

CartPole is also an unforgiving environment in the sense that recovery from a bad state is essentially impossible. Once the pole tilts past a small threshold, no action can save it. This means the agent has very little opportunity to observe the transition between near-failure and recovery, which is precisely the region of the state space where a useful policy must act. Algorithms that learn on-policy, such as PPO, accumulate this experience naturally by rolling out full trajectories and can gradually push into harder states as the policy improves. DQN, because it trains on stored transitions from a buffer dominated by early failures, does not benefit from this kind of progressive curriculum.

Together, these factors explain why DQN's failure on CartPole is consistent across every integrator and timestep. The numerical configuration of the simulator changes the specific trajectories the agent observes, but it does not change the fundamental dynamic that prevents the replay buffer from accumulating the quality of experience that DQN requires.
